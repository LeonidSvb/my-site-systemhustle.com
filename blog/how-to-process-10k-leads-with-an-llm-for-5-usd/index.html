How to Process 10,000 Leads With an LLM for $5

Run a real cold email campaign — 1,000 sends a day — and you need 10,000+ fresh, personalized leads every month. Here's how I got the AI cost for that down to $5 per 10k. Every dumb mistake included, providers named.

And forget generic "{{firstName}}, I saw your company" spam that gets zero replies. Real personalization means pulling actual variables per lead, summarizing what each company does, figuring out who's even worth emailing in the first place. That's not a copy-paste job — that's an AI job, at scale.

Everyone's heard of Claude Code by now. Some people still swear by n8n, which by this point is mostly a graveyard of broken workflows. But none of those, on their own, answer the real question. That took me a while — and a few embarrassing wrong turns — to figure out. Here's the whole thing, nothing skipped.

Wrong tool first, and that's fine

I actually started on my Claude Code subscription for this. Bad idea. It kept spinning up subagents on its own, burning through my usage limits fast, and then I'm sitting there for 5 hours waiting for the reset. That's when it clicked: Claude is the guy you bring in for the hard, judgment-heavy work — not the one you hire to process 10,000 rows of the same simple task. For that, you want cheap labor you can throw in bulk. So I moved to OpenRouter — one API, hundreds of models, pay per call, test anything.

Attempt one: I picked a number out of thin air

Default settings, basically no concurrency tuning — one request at a time. My prompt was big, so each call took close to a minute. Do the math on a few thousand leads and that's just not happening. So I bump concurrency up to 50 — 50 calls running at once instead of one. Better, but still slow on real volume. And in my head, at that point, 50 quietly became "the limit." Nobody told me that. I just stopped questioning it — which turned out to be the actual mistake, not the number itself.

Attempt two: chase the fastest

Next idea: OpenRouter has a "sort by fastest" option. I flip it on. It picks a provider called Cerebras and routes everything there. Speed's real — but the bill is insane, noticeably worse than before I touched anything.

Attempt three: chase the cheapest

Fine, I think, other direction — sort by cheapest. Lands on a provider called AkashML. And this is the one I'd actually tell as a story at a bar: I fire off a batch of just 30 domains, and it sits there for 15 minutes straight with zero finished results before it finally times out. Not slow. Just... gone.

The question I skipped the first time

At this point I stopped guessing settings and asked a better question: who actually said 50 was the right concurrency? Where did that ceiling come from? So I ran a real stress test — ramped concurrency up step by step and tracked error rate and speed at every step, logged into my own database so I could see the real pattern instead of eyeballing a terminal scrolling by. Without real tracking this is just vibes. With it, I could see exactly where things broke.

Quick tangent — why a server at all

Why does this even need a server, why not just run it locally and dump results into a folder of files? For a one-off test, it doesn't. But I've been down that road: a handful of JSON or, worse, CSV files, half of them with commas in the data breaking the parsing, spending an evening debugging why row 4,312 came back mangled. Never again.

What a real database actually buys you instead: everything lands in one place you can query, not files you have to hunt through by hand. A crash mid-batch doesn't lose the work already done. A budget cap can actually stop a bad setting from quietly burning through your API spend. And a re-run skips what it already paid for instead of doing it twice.

Past 400, throughput doesn't plateau — it drops by two-thirds, and almost a third of all calls need a retry. That's not diminishing returns, that's the provider choking. There's a real ceiling, invisible until you actually measure it — going past it doesn't just stop helping, it actively hurts.

The golden balance

Last piece: I went back to the fastest-vs-cheapest tradeoff, except now I understood it. So instead of picking a side, I put a price ceiling on top of "fastest" — basically telling it "give me the fastest option, but only among the cheap ones." That's the setting that landed on CoreWeave — same price as AkashML, the one that stalled, but wired into a wider pool of fallback providers instead of betting everything on one. Fast and reliable and cheap, because it's never stuck with only one door.

A note on honesty

The $5/10k number below is real — but it's real for my prompt, and mine is heavy. It's not a one-line extraction. Each call reads the merged text of every scraped page on a site and, in one shot, writes a full prose company summary plus fills out a dozen-plus structured fields — buyer type, services, differentiator, credentials, geography, deal direction, and more.

A shorter, lighter prompt would stretch the same $5 further than mine does. I just haven't measured that number, so I'm not going to make one up and hand it to you.

Nothing here was a model upgrade or a clever prompt trick. It was three dumb-sounding questions asked in order: why is this slow, why is this expensive, and who told me that number was the limit. Worth asking all three before you assume the tool itself is the problem.
