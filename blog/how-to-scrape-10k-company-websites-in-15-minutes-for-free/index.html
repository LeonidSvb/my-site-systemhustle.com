How to Scrape 10,000 Company Websites in 15 Minutes for Free

Some sites are just plain text — free to grab. Others need a real browser to even open. Every scraping tool charges you the same price either way. Most sites are the free kind. Here's how I stopped paying for them.

But the setup cost me a couple hours, once. Now I've got ready-to-use leads sitting in my database for a month or more, on demand, for free.

Here's where that came from. "Hi {{firstName}}, love what you're building" gets deleted in half a second. A line that actually shows you looked at the company gets read. So before my AI writes one word of an email, it needs real text about that company — every lead in the pipeline gets their site pulled first.

That text has to earn its place before it touches the copy, though. First question it answers: is this company even worth emailing? Consumer-facing, wrong size, a dozen other red flags — cut it now for a few cents, don't waste real time on it later. Even a booked call is a wasted call if the company was never a fit. Why not just Common Crawl, since it's free too

It's old. The snapshot on any given day is 29-42 days behind — a full crawl takes about two weeks to run, and indexes come out roughly monthly.

It's less complete, even on the easy sites. I checked the domains my free scraper already handles fine — the ones where staleness isn't even the issue — and Common Crawl only had two or more usable pages on 41% of them. It's not a superset of what own_http already gets for free; it's patchier, on the same easy domains.

Staleness isn't free on every page. For a static About page, a month-old copy is fine. For something time-sensitive — a careers page, a "we're hiring" signal — a month is enough for the role to be filled or the person to have already left. If you're using a page as a live signal, not just background text, old data can point you at something that's no longer true.

The "free" promo that cost me hours, not dollars

First real attempt at this: Firecrawl, a paid scraping tool. I found a free promo — real credit, zero dollars charged. I had three accounts, so I grabbed it on all three. I figured that meant three times the speed.

It didn't. They track you by IP, not by account — doesn't matter how many accounts you run, you're still stuck behind the same one door. What should've taken minutes took hours. Free wasn't free. It just charged me in time instead of money.

I hit that same wall again later, for real, inside the actual project — and this time I measured it instead of guessing. Real, sustained speed: about 5.5 sites a minute.

So I stopped renting permission to scrape

Then I noticed something working an old-school niche: most of these company sites are dumb-simple. No JavaScript app, no fancy framework — just plain HTML that shows up the second you ask for it.

Here's the part that should annoy you: every paid scraping tool charges the exact same price whether a site needs a full browser or not. You're paying moving-truck prices to carry one box.

So I built the thing that only carries the box. A scraper that just asks the site for its page, directly. No browser. No third-party tool. No rate limit — because it isn't sharing anyone else's budget.

Mine, free 662 domains/minute, live test, no rate limit — different target sites, not a shared API budget
Firecrawl, paid, honestly measured 5.5 domains/minute, sustained, on a real paid plan (their cap is ~20 requests/min, but each domain can eat up to 3 of them)

Do the math on the headline number: 662 domains a minute means 10,000 domains in about 15 minutes. Not overnight. Not a queue you check tomorrow morning. And it costs $0 — the only thing it spends is your own server's CPU.

The honest ceiling

This isn't a magic trick that works on 100% of the internet. I'm not going to pretend it is.

A real run across 23,427 domains landed at 76.1% usable on the free pass alone. Three out of every four sites, for nothing.

The rest genuinely need a real browser, or the site's blocked or dead. And this split is specific to the old-school niche I tested on — a more modern industry, like marketing agencies, will lean harder toward needing a browser. Your number will move.

The bug that made live sites look dead

Every batch had sites logged as "dead" that weren't dead at all — just misdiagnosed by a bottleneck buried in our own setup, nothing to do with the site itself. I checked one batch by hand: 5,797 of 8,068 "failed" sites were completely alive. One setting fixed it.

This is exactly the kind of landmine your coding agent should catch, not you. Copy this whole post to it before you build your own version — it'll know what to look for. No reason to make you learn the plumbing.

Silver lining: even before that bug was fixed, this cheap first pass was already paying for itself — a free way to tell which sites in a niche are simple and which need a real browser, before you spend a cent finding that out the expensive way. Just knowing that split saved me 20-30% of what I'd otherwise have paid downstream.

Nobody waits for the slow one

One more thing that makes 662 domains a minute possible: how many sites run at the same time, and what happens when one of them is slow. Too few at once and you're wasting time. Too many and sites start timing out on each other. We tested a range and landed on 40 at once. But the number alone isn't the trick — it's what happens the instant one finishes early.

Waiting for the group vs. never waiting — same 8 sites, same durations
Every bar is one site's real scrape time. The only thing that changes is how the next site gets picked up.

Top: everyone waits for the group. Bottom: nobody waits — the slow site only ever costs one slot, not the whole run.

One slow site should cost you one slot. If it's costing you the whole batch, you don't have a scraping problem — you have a scheduling problem.

Same rule, applied twice: never let capacity sit idle while there's work waiting for it. Whether that's a slot in your own scraper or spending room on a paid tool's per-minute limit doesn't matter — the fix is the same.

What actually made it faster and more reliable

A few practical fixes, kept short on purpose — this is plumbing, not something you need to understand, just know it exists:

Own HTTP scraper — what actually helped
  • Stopped rejecting sites with outdated security certificates. Recovered a pile of real, working sites — for free.
  • Every request gets a hard time limit, no exceptions. A stuck site never holds up the run.
  • Save results in small groups, not one at a time. Saving one-by-one crashed the whole thing once, not just that one job.
  • Read the page properly instead of a shortcut. Fixed junk text — leftover code, duplicated menus — sneaking into the results.

None of this is exotic. It's exactly the kind of thing your coding agent handles on its own if you just tell it to build this — copy this post over and it'll know what to look for.

Getting more than the homepage — for free

Homepage alone is thin. 5 to 7 pages a site — services, about, case studies — gives the AI real material to work with. More material, sharper copy. Sharper copy, better replies. If you can get more for free, get more. There's no reason not to.

Finding that list of pages is simple: some sites publish a sitemap — a built-in table of contents. The rest, you do it the way a person would — check the homepage for links to other pages, follow those a level or two deep. Some sites genuinely don't have more to give — dead ones, one-page placeholders. Not a tooling problem, just how much that site has.

One more quiet decision

I identify a lead by their email domain, not the "website" field on their record — people change jobs, that field goes stale, and guessing wrong scrapes the wrong company. Small fix, real impact: it kept about 3,250 leads from getting matched to a company they don't actually work at.

Same lesson, every section: measure before you build, don't pay for what you're not using. A plain HTTP request handles most of a niche for free. The rest is a real problem — worth solving, just not with your wallet first.

One practical note: I run this on my own server, not my laptop. It'll work locally too — nothing about it requires a server specifically — but a server stays on, doesn't sleep mid-run, and won't choke if you're doing something else on the same machine. Worth the adjustment if you're planning to run this regularly.
